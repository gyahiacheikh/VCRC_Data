This project focuses on the comprehensive analysis of violent crime trends in the state of California over the period 2000–2013, combining exploratory data analysis, statistical modeling, machine learning techniques, and geographic visualization. The primary objective is to understand how violent crime rates evolve over time and how they relate to key socio-economic and demographic factors such as population size and economic output (GDP), while also providing tools to compare counties with similar crime profiles.

The dataset integrates multiple sources of information at the county level, including recorded violent crime incidents, population figures, geographic coordinates, and GDP data. A significant part of the project involves data preprocessing and enrichment: cleaning raw crime records, normalizing crime rates per capita to allow fair comparisons across counties, and merging external datasets (GDP and geographic coordinates) to create a unified analytical framework. This preparation step ensures that both temporal and spatial analyses are statistically meaningful and interpretable.

The exploratory analysis phase examines long-term trends in violent crime across California, identifying patterns such as overall increases or decreases over time, differences between counties, and disparities between urban and rural regions. Statistical techniques are used to study correlations between crime rates, population size, and GDP, providing insight into whether economic activity or demographic scale is associated with higher or lower levels of violent crime. These analyses help contextualize crime not as an isolated phenomenon, but as one embedded in broader socio-economic structures.

Geographic visualization plays a central role in the project. By leveraging county-level coordinates, the analysis produces spatial representations of violent crime rates, allowing patterns to be observed geographically rather than only numerically. These visualizations make it possible to detect regional clusters, coastal versus inland differences, and persistent high- or low-crime areas, thereby enhancing interpretability for both technical and non-technical audiences.

Beyond descriptive analysis, the project incorporates predictive and similarity-based methods. Machine learning techniques are applied to model relationships between crime rates and explanatory variables, enabling exploratory prediction and pattern recognition. In addition, a recommender system is developed to identify counties with similar profiles based on crime, population, and economic indicators. This system allows users to compare counties with analogous characteristics, which can be valuable for policy analysis, benchmarking, or transferring insights from one region to another.

Overall, the project demonstrates an end-to-end data science workflow: from data acquisition and preprocessing, through exploratory and statistical analysis, to modeling, visualization, and decision-support tools. By combining quantitative rigor with geographic and comparative perspectives, the project provides a structured and interpretable approach to understanding violent crime dynamics in California and supports informed decision-making in contexts such as public policy, urban planning, and socio-economic research.
